A loan book is payments running both ways between the same institutions: repayments one way, new draws and interest the other, margin calls whenever prices move. Each one settles gross, so each needs liquidity in place on the day it falls due - and the days the most falls due are the volatile ones, when liquidity is scarcest.

## What Changes for You

* **Settle the book net.** A loan book is a netting set: repayments, draws, interest, and margin calls between the same participants clear on [one graph](/netting), and only each institution's residual is funded.
* **Liquidity freed when it is scarcest.** The gap between gross and net is widest in a high-volatility event, when gross settlement demands the most liquidity just as it is scarcest.
* **Your terms, unchanged.** Each obligation keeps its own due date as its deadline and clears in the run before it; nothing is novated, and every loan stays between the two parties who signed it.
* **The residual, handled.** Whatever doesn't net takes a priced backstop from [programmable collateral vaults](/collateral/vaults), with an early exit any time before the deadline - so the worst case is what you pay today.
* **Collateral you can actually liquidate.** A tokenized asset that can be [redeemed T+0](/collateral/instant-redemption) is one you can list as collateral.

## A Day of Flow

Three institutions, one settlement date, USDC:

* The Fund owes the Bank **100** - a loan repayment
* The Bank owes the Desk **70** - a margin call on a hedge
* The Desk owes the Fund **50** - interest and a new draw

Gross, that is 220 USDC across three payments. Netted, the loop of 50 cancels, and the rest settles along the original debts: the Fund pays the Bank 50, the Bank pays the Desk 20. **70 moves instead of 220.**

## How It Enters

Each repayment, draw, or margin call enters as a **signed debt** from the institution that owes it, or straight from the lending market that originated it; from there it is the standard [integration](/netting-api/integration#submit-obligations).
